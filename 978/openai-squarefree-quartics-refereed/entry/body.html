The problem. Erdős problem #978 [Er65b, p. 219; Er81h, p. 178; see also Er53]. Write d for the degree (the problem page writes k). Erdős proved in 1953 that an irreducible integer polynomial of degree d ≥ 3 takes infinitely many (d−1)-power-free values, apart from a 2-adic exception when d is a power of 2. Hooley proved in 1967 that it does so with positive density. One power lower, nothing was known in small degree. Erdős wrote in 1953: "I have also not been able to prove, for example, that n4+2 is squarefree for infinitely many n" (p. 425). In 1965: "I could prove nothing about the representation of (k − 2)-th power-free numbers, for example, I cannot show that n4 + 2 represents infinitely many squarefree numbers" (§6, p. 219). In 1981: "there is no quartic polynomial about which we can prove that it represents infinitely many squarefree integers" (p. 178). The problem page asks: if d > 3 and no prime p has pd−2 dividing every value f(n), are there infinitely many n with f(n) (d−2)-power-free? In particular, is n4+2 squarefree infinitely often?

The local condition was added to the page on 31 March 2026. Moritz Firsching had posted a counterexample to the question without it, found by DeepMind's prover agent: x6+33x5+21x4+63x3+18x2+24x+48. It is congruent to x(x−1)⋯(x−5) mod 16, so 24 divides every value.

Before this release the asymptotic for r-power-free values was known for these ranges of the exponent r:

  • r ≥ (√2 − ½)d (Nair 1976), which reaches r = d−2 only for d ≥ 24;
  • r ≥ (3d+2)/4 (Heath-Brown 2006, Theorem 16), so d ≥ 10;
  • r ≥ (3d+1)/4 (Browning 2011), so d ≥ 9.

Two other results bear on it:

  • For binomials xd+c only, r ≥ (5d+3)/9 suffices (Heath-Brown 2013), so r = d−2 for d ≥ 6. Heath-Brown wrote there that "the most interesting open question then concerns quartic polynomials taking square-free values".
  • Granville (1998) obtained squarefree values in every case, but conditionally on the abc conjecture.

Our July report on #978 (/report/978) found the problem open. It counted 75,670 squarefree values of n4+2 for n ≤ 105, with every factorisation checked independently. It enclosed the predicted density between 0.75668370 and 0.75668401, and found a sign error in the decisive step of the one public claim then on record (Carella, arXiv 2310.16952).

It also named "the exact unresolved range" as primes p > √X. That was wrong. The elementary count Σp≤X ρ(p2)(X/p2 + 1) = O(X/Y) + O(π(X)), taken over primes p > Y, already handles every p ≤ X; this is the paper's (2.5). So the open range was p > X, which is exactly OpenAI's Proposition 1.4. The report now carries a dated correction.

The claim. OpenAI, "Squarefree values of quartics and power-free values of polynomials" (dated 24 September 2026, 55 pages, public since 6 October as family 020 of github.com/openai/math). In the paper's notation k = d − 2 is the exponent.

Theorem 1.1: let f ∈ ℤ[x] be irreducible over ℚ of degree d with 4 ≤ d ≤ 8, and suppose ρf(pk) < pk for every prime p, so that no prime k-th power divides every value. Then

#{1 ≤ n ≤ X : f(n) is k-power-free} = cf,kX + o(X), cf,k = ∏p(1 − ρf(pk)/pk) > 0.

No primitivity or sign condition is needed. The rest of the paper:

  • Theorem 1.1 rests on Proposition 1.4, the large-prime tail: #{X < n ≤ 2X : pk | f(n) for some prime p > X} ≪ X1−δ.
  • Corollary 1.2 extends Theorem 1.1 to every d ≥ 4, citing Browning (in Xiao's form) for d ≥ 9.
  • Corollary 1.3 does the same for products and families of polynomials whose irreducible factors have degree at most k+2. Its proof also cites Reuss and Xiao.

For #978 this answers both parts that were open. Every irreducible quartic with no fixed square divisor takes squarefree values with the predicted positive density. n4+2 is one: it is Eisenstein at 2, and f(0) = 2 is divisible by no prime square. The page's (d−2)-question is answered for every degree d ≥ 4, with density rather than just infinitude, and without the page's restriction that d is not a power of 2 or its positive leading coefficient.

The argument. The paper follows Reuss's framework for power-free values: factorisation in a number field, plus approximate determinants. It adds new pieces.

  • §2 reduces the density to Proposition 1.4. It uses a finite sieve for small primes, the bound ρ(pk) ≤ d (by Hensel, outside a finite set of primes) for primes between the cut-off and X, and the tail for primes above X. The limit in X is taken before the sieve cut-off goes to infinity.
  • §3 proves a uniform affine counting theorem. It counts integer points of bounded weighted size on algebraic sets whose equations may change from box to box, given lower bounds on the weighted Hilbert functions of their subvarieties. The proof cuts residue classes at auxiliary primes. A random deletion argument handles the primes at which later, moving equations fail to give smooth local coordinates.
  • §4 factors the ideal (n−θ) in K = ℚ(θ) as αkβ = μ(n−θ), with balanced conjugates of sizes P1/d and Xb/d. The integer coordinates of α and β fill two boxes.
  • §§5–9 cut the paired boxes by archimedean determinants, change to reduced lattice coordinates, and compute weighted Hilbert bounds. They add explicit surface and curve geometry for quartics. On a curve the extreme case is a quintic polynomial with four prescribed critical values. Its integer values form one set of size O(X1/5), which is deleted once, before any box is counted (Proposition 8.2).
  • §10 and Appendix A choose the parameters and check the exponent margins with an exact finite certificate.
  • §11 assembles the tail. For large η = log p/log X it counts directly on the surface f(x) = yzk (§11.1). Elsewhere it uses the number-field count (§11.2). Every box exponent there is below 24979/25000, and every removed-fibre exponent in the quartic mode is below 3122/3125. These are uniform bounds from the 999/1000 certificate; Table 1's are stronger.

The Lean, and our replay. OpenAI's comparator challenge (ComparatorChallenges/PowerFreeValues.lean) states OAI.QuarticPowerFree.allDegrees, which is Corollary 1.2. Take f ∈ ℤ[x] irreducible over ℚ with natDegree ≥ 4 and LocallyAdmissible f (natDegree − 2). Then:

  • the local factors are multipliable;
  • their product is positive;
  • count − c·X = o(X) as real X → ∞.

The Lean does not follow the paper. Seat L read all 63 files against the paper. The closure has no comments, so its map of files to sections is by content.

What the kernel-checked proof shares with the paper:

  • the sieve transfer (§2) and the balanced factorisation of Lemma 4.1;
  • an affine counting theorem of Theorem 3.1's shape, which is itself a determinant method with cuts at auxiliary primes;
  • weighted Hilbert thresholds of §7's shape;
  • quartic surface and curve alternatives of its own, in the spirit of §§8–9.

Then it takes a shorter route:

  • No archimedean cut. It never makes the archimedean (Taylor) determinant cut of §5. Instead it applies the counting theorem to the whole (d+1)-dimensional norm variety in ℤ2d. The coordinates are those of α and β in a fixed integral basis, and the equations are aikbi − a0kb0 = θ0 − θi. This needs a top-dimensional threshold the paper never states.
  • Exceptional fibres, arithmetically. A fixed n gives one point. A fixed α gives at most a bounded number, since ρ(pk) ≤ d and pk > X. For "radial" fibres α′ = cα, prime norms force c = ±1.
  • Its own certificates. For 5 ≤ d ≤ 8 it uses one box for the whole range of η, with exponents U = 1/3 and V = 2/5. For d = 4 it uses 100 bins in η, with a certificate checked by kernel decide. The tail saving is X−3/1000 for 4 ≤ d ≤ 8. L re-checked every certificate inequality in exact arithmetic, and all hold. The closest calls: for d = 5 the exponent sum meets its 997/1000 bound exactly (allowed, since the inequality is ≤), and the full-dimension thresholds clear their envelopes by as little as 0.00049 (d = 7) and 0.00059 (d = 4, first bin).
  • d ≥ 9 without Browning. It proves d ≥ 9 itself, using the paper's own §11.1 device: the surface f(x) = yzk in two weight boxes, with saving X−1/2000. No result of Browning, Xiao, Heath-Brown or Reuss enters anywhere. L notes the paper could have done the same: on [1, d/k], η(1 − kη/d) is largest at η = 1, where it is 2/d ≤ 2/9, well inside the range §11.1 handles.

So the Lean needs none of the following: Lemma 4.2, §§5–6, Proposition 8.2 (the quintic deletion), Proposition 10.1, Appendix A, §11.1 in degrees d ≤ 8, or Browning's theorem. Its quartic surface bound is its own analogue of Proposition 8.1, with different alternatives. Two things follow.

  • If the replay is right, no objection to those parts of the paper can sink Theorem 1.1, Corollary 1.2 or #978.
  • The Lean cannot vouch for those parts either. They stand on the paper alone, and so do Corollary 1.3 and §12, which have no formal counterpart.

Formal Conjectures' three statements of #978, proved in Lean (ours). Formal Conjectures has two statements tagged "research solved" since PR #6898 (merged 11 October), each with a link to OpenAI's allDegrees:

  • Erdos978.erdos_978.parts.ii: infinitely many (d−2)-power-free values;
  • parts.iii: {n | Squarefree (n4+2)} is infinite.

Their proofs are still sorry. So is that of variants.sub_two, which is Browning's d ≥ 9 case. Our file Bridge978.lean proves verbatim copies of all three from OpenAI's theorem. A script checks the copies against FC at a7b87c8d, and a mutated copy fails the check. The steps:

  • Irreducible in ℤ[X] with positive degree gives primitive, and Gauss's lemma then gives irreducible over ℚ.
  • FC's local condition (for every p, some n ≥ 0 with pk ∤ f(n)) gives ρ(pk) < pk, by reducing n mod pk.
  • A positive density makes the count unbounded, so the set is infinite.
  • OpenAI's PowerFree (no prime k-th power divides a) and FC's Powerfree (xk | a forces x to be a unit) give the same set.
  • For parts.iii, X4+2 is irreducible by Eisenstein at 2, local admissibility comes from f(0) = 2, and Squarefree in ℕ is matched to PowerFree 2 in ℤ.

The file also proves the density form in FC's own vocabulary. FC currently states density only for Hooley's (d−1) case (parts.i). Our form: {n | Powerfree (d−2) (f(n))} has natural density equal to OpenAI's Euler product, in FC's Set.HasDensity (which counts 0 ≤ n < b), and so it has positive density.

This form needs neither the restriction that d is not a power of 2 nor a sign condition. That matters because FC's parts.ii keeps the page's preamble excluding powers of 2. Read literally, the preamble removes every power-of-two degree (4, 8, 16, …), including 4 and 8 from Theorem 1.1's new range. Quartics enter FC only through parts.iii.

The seven top-level bridge theorems (the file's other six are lemmas they use) depend only on propext, Classical.choice and Quot.sound under --trust=0. Three mutants behave as they should:

  • With the conclusion's exponent d−2 changed to d−3, the proof fails at the step that uses the exponent.
  • With the local condition dropped, it fails where it tries to supply local admissibility. That statement is false: DeepMind's degree-6 polynomial is a counterexample.
  • With a sorry planted in the density-to-infinitude lemma, sorryAx appears in the axioms of all three FC theorems.
