The question, and both rules. Start from a finite set of positive integers and extend it greedily: the next term is the least integer above the last one that is not a sum of two earlier terms. Erdős–Graham (1980, p. 53), following Dickson, allow the two earlier terms to coincide (i, j ≤ n; call this rule D) and ask whether the gaps am+1 − am are always eventually periodic. Zhiheng Li (August 2026, with GPT-5.6 Sol, Lean 4) answered no; we refereed his disproof on 13 October. Ben Green's open-problems list (the comments to Problem 7, January 2026) poses the same question for Queneau's variant, rule Q: the next term is the least integer above the last that is not ui + uj with i < j ≤ n — two distinct earlier terms — and writes that "for both variants, the following question appears to be open: is un+1 − un eventually periodic?" Li's identities need the doubles: at the ends of his blocks of quotients the only representation of a sum is 2u with u on the same rail, and rule Q removes exactly those; under rule Q his seed settles into a period (membership period 5,733 = 49 · 117, certified by our finite certificate on 13 October). So his seed does not settle the variant.

The theorem. Let

A = {1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 13, 22, 27, 28, 32, 36, 40, 47, 48, 52, 56, 63, 71, 81, 89, 97}

— Li's seed with 77 replaced by 56. Under rule Q the greedy extension of A has a gap sequence that is not eventually periodic. Under rule D it does not either. So under Queneau's variant of #341 not every seed gives eventually periodic gaps, and A is also a 21-element counterexample to #341 itself outside Li's infinite family (his seeds Ap, p ≥ 18, have 7p − 20 ≥ 106 elements).

How it was found: the greedy is its own fixed-point solver. Li's extension is five full residue classes mod 49 (B = {3, 22, 32, 40, 48}), seven small exceptions (D = {1, 2, 5, 13, 27, 36, 47}), and three rails 49q + 7, 49q + 14, 49q + 28 whose membership is switched by sets Y0, Y1, Y2 of quotients q. His shield lemma mod 49 makes the rails hear only each other: a rail integer is a sum of two earlier terms exactly when its quotient is a sum of two quotients on the previous rail (7 + 7 → 14, 14 + 14 → 28, 28 + 28 → 7 with a carry of one). Under rule D this forces Y1 = ℕ0 ∖ (Y0 + Y0), Y2 = ℕ0 ∖ (Y1 + Y1), Y0 = 1 + ℕ0 ∖ (Y2 + Y2) for every quotient q ≥ 2, and Li's blocks of quotients [4 · 8k, 8 · 8k − 1] satisfy these exactly. Under rule Q the sumsets lose their doubles (write X ⊕ X for sums of two distinct elements), the self-sumset of an interval shrinks by one at both ends, and Li's intervals no longer close up; a first attempt to keep his block positions and solve for integer offsets that do close found none (the ansatz and its dead end are in the bundle's note). But nothing has to be designed: once the seed's rail content for q ≤ 1 is fixed, the three gates force every later quotient, so the greedy computes its own controller. There are 27 seeds D ∪ B ∪ (49 + B) ∪ R with R ⊆ {7, 56, 14, 63, 28, 77} meeting every rail at q ≤ 1 (the cover step of the shield needs a representative on each rail). Running the forced dynamics from each to 32,768 quotients: 18 show no period up to 8,192 on the window [16,384, 32,768) under rule Q (and 25 under rule D), Li's own R = {7, 28, 63, 77} being periodic with period 117 under rule Q; and for all 27, under rule Q, an independent greedy on the integers agrees with the rail model at every integer to 106. One of the 18, R = {7, 28, 56, 63}, produces a controller clean enough to prove by hand.

The strict controller. With Ik = [8k + 1, 2 · 8k], Jk = [4 · 8k, 8 · 8k], Kk = [2 · 8k, 4 · 8k] (k ≥ 0, so I0 = {2}, J0 = [4, 8], K0 = [2, 4]):

Y0 = {0, 1} ∪ ⋃k≥0 Ik,   Y1 = {1} ∪ ⋃k≥0 Jk,   Y2 = {0} ∪ ⋃k≥0 Kk.

The quotients 0 and 1 are the seed's: 7, 56 ∈ A on the 7-rail (0, 1 ∈ Y0), 63 ∈ A and 14 ∉ A on the 14-rail (Y1 ∩ {0, 1} = {1}), 28 ∈ A and 77 ∉ A on the 28-rail (Y2 ∩ {0, 1} = {0}). Set S = D ∪ {49q + b : q ≥ 0, b ∈ B} ∪ {49q + 7 : q ∈ Y0} ∪ {49q + 14 : q ∈ Y1} ∪ {49q + 28 : q ∈ Y2}; then S ∩ [1, 97] = A.

Lemma 2 (the strict sumsets). (S0) Y0 ⊕ Y0 = {1, 2, 3} ∪ ⋃k≥1 [8k + 1, 4 · 8k − 1];  (S1) Y1 ⊕ Y1 = ⋃k≥0 [4 · 8k + 1, 16 · 8k − 1];  (S2) Y2 ⊕ Y2 = ⋃k≥0 [2 · 8k, 8 · 8k − 1].
Proof. Group the sums of two distinct elements by the larger block involved. (S0): {0, 1} ⊕ {0, 1} = {1}; {0, 1} + Ik = [8k + 1, 2 · 8k + 1]; for k ≥ 1, Ik ⊕ Ik = [2 · 8k + 3, 4 · 8k − 1] (least sum (8k + 1) + (8k + 2), greatest (2 · 8k − 1) + 2 · 8k), while I0 ⊕ I0 = ∅; for j < k, Ij + Ik ⊆ [8k + 8j + 2, 2 · 8k + 2 · 8j], and with j = 0 this is [8k + 3, 2 · 8k + 2], which supplies the one missing point 2 · 8k + 2; for 1 ≤ j < k it lies inside [8k + 1, 2 · 8k + 2 · 8k−1] ⊂ [8k + 1, 4 · 8k − 1]. So for k ≥ 1 the sums whose larger summand is in Ik fill exactly [8k + 1, 4 · 8k − 1], the sums inside {0, 1} ∪ I0 are {1, 2, 3}, and 4 · 8k − 1 < 8k+1 + 1 keeps the pieces apart. (S1): 1 + Jk = [4 · 8k + 1, 8 · 8k + 1]; Jk ⊕ Jk = [8 · 8k + 1, 16 · 8k − 1]; for j < k, Jj + Jk ⊆ [4 · 8k + 4, 8 · 8k + 8k] ⊂ [4 · 8k + 1, 16 · 8k − 1]; the lone element 1 makes no sum by itself; and 16 · 8k − 1 < 4 · 8k+1 + 1. (S2): 0 + Kk = Kk; Kk ⊕ Kk = [4 · 8k + 1, 8 · 8k − 1]; for j < k, Kj + Kk ⊆ [2 · 8k + 2, 4 · 8k + 4 · 8k−1] ⊂ [2 · 8k, 8 · 8k − 1]; and 8 · 8k − 1 < 2 · 8k+1. ∎

Lemma 3 (the gates). For every q ≥ 2: (G1) q ∈ Y1 ⟺ q ∉ Y0 ⊕ Y0;  (G2) q ∈ Y2 ⟺ q ∉ Y1 ⊕ Y1;  (G3) q ∈ Y0 ⟺ q − 1 ∉ Y2 ⊕ Y2.
Proof. By (S0), ℕ0 ∖ (Y0 ⊕ Y0) = {0} ∪ [4, 8] ∪ ⋃k≥1 [4 · 8k, 8k+1] = {0} ∪ ⋃k≥0 Jk, which agrees with Y1 on [2, ∞). By (S1), ℕ0 ∖ (Y1 ⊕ Y1) = [0, 4] ∪ ⋃k≥0 [16 · 8k, 32 · 8k] = [0, 4] ∪ ⋃k≥1 Kk, which agrees with Y2 on [2, ∞) since K0 = [2, 4]. By (S2), ℕ0 ∖ (Y2 ⊕ Y2) = {0, 1} ∪ ⋃k≥1 [8k, 2 · 8k − 1], so for q ≥ 2, q − 1 lies in it exactly when q ∈ {2} ∪ ⋃k≥1 [8k + 1, 2 · 8k] = Y0 ∩ [2, ∞). The values at q = 0, 1 are the seed's and are not governed by the gates. ∎

The shield, and the recurrence under rule Q. Li's Lemma 3.1 (a finite check in ℤ/49, recomputed): with P = {7, 14, 28} and Q = P ∪ B, (cover) (B+B) ∪ (P+B) ∪ (D+B) = ℤ/49 ∖ Q; (avoid) Q meets none of B+B, P+B, D+B, D+P; (PP) (P+P) ∩ Q = P, the only ordered pairs of P × P with sum in Q being 7 + 7, 14 + 14, 28 + 28. Theorem 3. For every t > 97: t ∈ S ⟺ t ∉ S ⊕ S. Proof. Write t = 49q + r; t > 97 gives q ≥ 2. If r ∈ Q: a D+D sum is ≤ 94 < t; (avoid) excludes the types D+B, D+P, B+B, B+P whether or not the summands coincide; mixed P+P sums have residues 21, 35, 42 ∉ Q; so only the three diagonal rail pairs reach Q, and two distinct elements of one rail have distinct quotients, whence 49q + 14 ∈ S ⊕ S ⟺ q ∈ Y0 ⊕ Y0, 49q + 28 ∈ S ⊕ S ⟺ q ∈ Y1 ⊕ Y1, and 49q + 7 ∈ S ⊕ S ⟺ q − 1 ∈ Y2 ⊕ Y2 (since (49u + 28) + (49v + 28) = 49(u + v + 1) + 7). Lemma 3 then says every rail integer t > 97 is in S exactly when it is not in S ⊕ S; every B-rail integer is in S and, by (avoid) and (PP), not in S ⊕ S. If r ∉ Q: t ∉ S. By (cover) choose x ∈ B ∪ D ∪ P and b ∈ B with x + b ≡ r, and take the representative ex = x for x ∈ B ∪ D, e7 = 7, e14 = 63, e28 = 28 — all in A ⊂ S. With ex = 49ux + x and x + b = 49c + r (ux, c ∈ {0, 1}) and q ≥ 2, the number 49(q − ux − c) + b is a positive element of the b-rail and t = ex + (49(q − ux − c) + b). The two summands are distinct — this is the one new point rule Q needs: their residues are x and b, and B, D, P are disjoint sets of residues, so equality would force x = b ∈ B, ex = x ≤ 48, against the other summand being ≥ 49 + b. So t ∈ S ⊕ S. ∎ Li's Lemma 1.2 with ⊕ for + (every representation t = u + v, u ≠ v ∈ S, has u, v < t, so the scan from 98 selects exactly S) now says the rule-Q extension of A is S.

No period. 49q + 7 ∈ S ⟺ q ∈ Y0, and Y0 has 8k consecutive members ([8k + 1, 2 · 8k]) followed by 6 · 8k consecutive non-members. If membership in S were ultimately periodic with period h, then 49h would be a period too, and since 49(q + h) + 7 = (49q + 7) + 49h, h would be an ultimate period of the indicator of Y0; a run of h or more non-members inside the periodic tail would force that tail to vanish, against the later blocks. So the indicator of S is not ultimately periodic, and Li's Lemma 4.1 (eventually periodic gaps give an ultimately periodic indicator, the period being the sum of one period of gaps) finishes: the gaps are not eventually periodic. That is Theorem 1.

The same seed under rule D (Theorem 2). With coincident summands allowed the forced controller is Y0′ = {0, 1} ∪ ⋃k≥0 [2 · 8k, 4 · 8k − 1], Y1′ = {1} ∪ ⋃k≥1 [8k − 1, 2 · 8k − 1], Y2′ = {0} ∪ ⋃k≥0 [4 · 8k − 1, 8 · 8k − 1] — the same seed content at q ≤ 1, different blocks (Li's own were [4 · 8k, 8 · 8k − 1], [16 · 8k − 1, 32 · 8k − 1], [64 · 8k − 1, 128 · 8k − 1]). The same interval calculation gives Y0′ + Y0′ = [0, 6] ∪ ⋃k≥1 [2 · 8k, 8 · 8k − 2], Y1′ + Y1′ = {2} ∪ ⋃k≥1 [8k, 4 · 8k − 2], Y2′ + Y2′ = {0} ∪ ⋃k≥0 [4 · 8k − 1, 16 · 8k − 2] (each: the block plus {0, 1} or {1} or {0}, the block plus itself, and cross terms of two blocks, which stay interior), whose complements agree with Y1′, Y2′ and Y0′ − 1 on [2, ∞) (so q ∈ Y0′ exactly when q − 1 is not a sum, as before); Li’s Theorem 3.2 is then verbatim (the distinctness remark is not needed), and Y0′ has runs of 2 · 8k members and 12 · 8k non-members. The full calculation is written out in the bundle's PROOF.md; only the rule-Q identities are proved on this page.

What the computer checked, by different routes. (1) From the closed forms alone, by FFT pair counts on [0, 87) = [0, 2,097,152): the three gate identities hold for q ≥ 2 under the rule they are claimed for (0 violations each), fail under the other rule (7, 7 and 6 violations), and fail when the start of the third block on any one rail is moved down by one (three mutations, each breaking at least one gate); the shield is recomputed; S ∩ [1, 97] = A; every t = 49q + r with r ∉ Q and 2 ≤ q < 60 has a cover pair of two distinct positive elements of S. (2) Two implementations of the forced rail dynamics (Python/numpy and C bitsets) reproduce the closed forms exactly from the seed's rail content, to 217 under both rules and to 220 under rule Q; as positive control, Li's own rail content under rule D reproduces his Y0, Y1, Y2 with 0 mismatches to 32,768, and under rule Q comes out periodic with period 117. (3) The independent bitset greedy on the integers written for the 13 October referee (it knows nothing about rails) run from A to 107 under each rule: under rule Q the greedy's 1,224,510 elements up to 107 coincide with the closed form S at every integer (0 mismatches), and under rule D its 1,224,509 elements coincide with S′. Its own probe over the second half of the gaps (612,255 of them under rule Q, 612,254 under rule D) finds no period up to half that length; over the last tenth under rule Q it finds a local period 6 — as it must while the quotients run through one block of the 14-rail (J5 = [131,072, 262,144] covers 107/49), where the live set is the five B-rails plus the whole 14-rail — which is exactly why no finite window can see this and a proof is needed. (4) The rail model run from the seed's rail content (route 2) agrees with the integer greedy under rule Q to 107 for A, and to 106 for all 27 seeds of the census.

Theorems 1 and 2 are (a) elementary: the rule-Q sumset identities are proved by hand above and the rule-D ones in the bundle's PROOF.md, the shield is a 49-residue table anyone can check in minutes, and the computer runs are controls, not the proof. The census of 27 seeds (18 with no period up to 8,192 on [16,384, 32,768) under rule Q, 25 under rule D) is (d) computer observation — only R = {7, 28, 56, 63} is proved, and the window criterion is weak: on A itself the verdict hinges on a single quotient (16,385, the first quotient after K4 ends — J4 starts at 16,384), so a window that missed a block boundary would read "period 1"; the referee reproduced the 18 and 25, with the same per-seed periods, from its own integer greedy on a different window ([10,204, 20,408), periods up to 2,550). What is ours and what is not. The shield mod 49, the three-rail scale-8 controller idea, and the two framing lemmas are Li's; Dickson's problem is settled by him.
