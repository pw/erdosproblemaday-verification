The problem (erdosproblems.com/341; Erdős–Graham 1980, p. 53; discussed in the comments to Problem 7 on Green's list). Start from a finite set
A = {a1 < … < ak} of positive integers and extend it greedily: for n ≥ k, an+1 is the least integer exceeding
an that is not of the form ai + aj with i, j ≤ n. Is the sequence of differences
an+1 − an always eventually periodic? Erdős and Graham write that this "old problem of Dickson [Dic (34)] is still wide open" and that
"even a starting set as small as {1, 4, 9, 16, 25} requires thousands of terms before periodicity eventually occurs" (our OCR of the monograph, p. 53, is in the bundle; Dickson's paper is
"The converse of Waring's problem", Bull. AMS 40 (1934) 711–714). One convention point matters for everything below and we settled it from the sources: Dickson's rule, as Erdős–Graham state it,
takes i, j ≤ n with i = j allowed — the Ulam problem in the very next paragraph of the monograph says "i ≠ j" explicitly, this one does not —
and Green's list says the same ("not of the form ui + uj for i, j ≤ n. This construction was considered by Dickson. A variant construction
considered by Queneau is to define un+1 to be the smallest integer greater than un and not of the form ui + uj with
i < j ≤ n. For both variants, the following question appears to be open"). The Formal Conjectures statement erdos_341 quantifies the forbidden sums as
{ai + aj | i ≤ n, j ≤ n}, equal indices included. So does the claim we are refereeing.

The claim on the tab. The proof-claims tab of erdosproblems.com/341 carries exactly one entry, submitted 9 August 2026 (15:32:18, the site's clock) by Zhiheng Li, "using GPT-5.6 Sol":
Counterexamples to Erdős Problem 341, a paper with a Lean 4 formalization (repository LiAlreadyExists/Erdos-341; the claim links the repository's main, which has been revised since the
claim was filed — the family of §5 arrived with v1.3.0 on 16 August — and we referee what main pointed to on 13 October: commit c1d9121 of 20 August 2026, tag v2.1.0,
Zenodo 10.5281/zenodo.22030579; the paper itself cites the v2.0.1 deposit, 10.5281/zenodo.22030511; the Lean files are byte-identical across the two). The answer is NO. For the 21-element seed
A = {1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 13, 22, 27, 28, 32, 36, 40, 47, 48, 52, 63, 71, 77, 81, 89, 97} the greedy extension has a consecutive-gap sequence that is not eventually periodic;
and §5 gives an infinite family Ap (one for every p ≥ 18, with 7p − 20 elements, modulus 7p) of further counterexamples. The page itself has not
changed since 20 January 2026 and shows no resolution (the site no longer displays statuses); the teorth database says OPEN (status last updated 31 August 2025; its "formalized" field was set
22 January 2026); but Formal Conjectures merged
"Erdős 341: the answer is no (Li 2026), link the formal disproof" on 18 September 2026 (PR #6070) and marks erdos_341 research solved, with answer(False).
So the problem has been answered in the public record — by a reader with a model, two months ago, in a preprint that no journal and nobody in public has refereed. This entry is that referee
report. Nothing in the disproof is ours.

What the construction is, in one paragraph. Li does not run the greedy algorithm and watch it; he writes the final set down in closed form and proves it obeys the rule.
Work modulo 49. Five residue classes are fully in: B = {3, 22, 32, 40, 48} (every 49q + b). Seven small integers D = {1, 2, 5, 13, 27, 36, 47} are in by fiat
(the part of the seed that lies on no class; the seed is D ∪ B ∪ (49 + B) ∪ {7, 28, 63, 77}). Three more classes, the "rails" P = {7, 14, 28}, are in at quotient q exactly when q lies in one of three sets built from powers of eight:
49q + 7 ∈ S iff q ∈ Y0 = {0} ∪ ⋃k [4·8k, 8·8k);
49q + 14 ∈ S iff q ∈ Y1 = [1, 3] ∪ ⋃k [16·8k − 1, 32·8k);
49q + 28 ∈ S iff q ∈ Y2 = [0, 1] ∪ [7, 15] ∪ ⋃k [64·8k − 1, 128·8k).
Why 7, 14, 28: doubling cycles them (8·7 ≡ 7 mod 49, because 2 has order 3 mod 7), and of all pair sums of rail residues only the
diagonal ones land in Q — 7 + 7 = 14, 14 + 14 = 28, 28 + 28 = 56 ≡ 7 (mod 49) — so the three rails
form a closed loop in which each one's membership at q is "not a pair-sum of the previous rail's quotients": Y1 = (Y0 + Y0)c,
Y2 = (Y1 + Y1)c, Y0 = {0} ∪ (1 + (Y2 + Y2)c) — three interval
calculations, since sums of unions of intervals are unions of intervals (the "scale-eight controller", Lemma 2.1). Everything else is a finite check — residues mod 49 plus a few small carries (the "shield", Lemma 3.1, and the small
representatives 7, 63, 28 that the controller's initial values put into S): the sums
B+B, P+B, D+B cover every residue outside Q = B ∪ P, none of B+B, P+B, D+B,
D+P lands in Q, and P+P meets Q only in the three diagonal pairs. Together (Theorem 3.2): for every t > 97,
t ∈ S ⇔ t ∉ S + S — which, scanned upward from 98, is the greedy rule (Lemma 1.2: a representation of t as a sum of two positive elements of
S uses only elements below t, so "not a sum of two earlier terms" and "not in S + S" coincide) — and S ∩ [1, 97] is exactly the seed. Aperiodicity is then the
easy half: the 7-rail copies Y0, whose member-blocks [4·8k, 8·8k) and the gaps between them (24·8k long) both grow
geometrically, so no period h survives past 8k > h (Lemma 2.2); and gaps eventually periodic would force membership eventually periodic (Lemma 4.1:
h periodic gaps give tm+h = tm + H for a fixed H). That is the whole proof. Every step is finite arithmetic or an
interval identity; there is no asymptotic estimate anywhere.

How we checked it. (i) Our own greedy, from the seed, knowing nothing of the construction. A 50-line C bitset program (greedy341.c,
in the bundle) applies Dickson's rule to the seed up to 107: 1,224,508 terms, density 0.12245 (≈ 6/49), largest gap 19, above the seed the five B-classes each hold 204,079–204,080
terms and the rails 91,734 / 74,903 / 37,453. Then, in a separate script (check_li.py), Li's closed-form S — transcribed from his Lean definitions, not from the paper's
prose — evaluated at every integer up to 107: 1,224,508 members, 0 mismatches against the greedy set, and S ∩ [1, 97] is the seed. The same script
recomputes the shield (the cover identity — 41 residues — the avoidance identity, the P+P identity and the diagonal-pairs claim) and the three controller identities on all quotients below 87; four
mutations (drop a B-residue; widen every Y0 block by an eighth; flip one bit of the greedy set; drop 14 from P) each make it fail, with the mismatch count printed
(204,081 / 4,681 / 1 / shield false). One thing worth seeing in the data: on the last quarter of [1, 107] the gap sequence has exact period 6 — that stretch lies inside a single
controller block (quotients 131,072 to 204,081, where the 7-rail is on and the others off) — while over the last half no period up to 306,127 fits. Our program's probe dutifully labels the
tail "periodic-compatible"; the label is meaningless, for or against. Aperiodicity lives at the scale transitions 8k, and a 107 window contains one of them.
A finite run cannot see this set's aperiodicity; it can only fail to find a period.
(ii) Kernel replay of the Lean. Fresh clone at c1d9121, the author's own pins (Lean v4.33.0-rc1, Mathlib tag v4.33.0-rc1; a tagged release-candidate with a published cache, so lake exe cache get
fetched 8,643 files in 52 s — on its second attempt; the first died when this box ran out of disk mid-download), lake build: 8,663 jobs, 0 errors (Erdos341Shield 86 s — the kernel-decided residue certificate — Erdos341 15 s,
Erdos341g 101 s). Our own audit file prints, with pp.all, the final theorem and the five definitions it is built from (GreedyExtension, LeastGreedyStep,
PrefixPairSum, EventuallyPeriodic, PairSum), prints Seed, S, enumeration and gap plainly, and the axioms of five declarations:
Erdos341.erdos_341_negative, S_is_greedy, least_next_rule_after_fixed_seed, gap_not_eventuallyPeriodic and the family theorem
Erdos3417p.theorem_5_1_nonperiodicity each depend on exactly [propext, Classical.choice, Quot.sound]; a negative control (a sorry'd copy of the
aperiodicity statement) prints sorryAx; a scan of the three first-party sources finds no sorry, admit, axiom, native_decide,
implemented_by, extern, unsafe, partial or opaque; leanchecker re-checks the two named modules Erdos341 and Erdos341g (46 s; a non-existent module
exits 1) — in that mode it replays only the modules named, so the Shield module, where the kernel-decided residue certificate lives, is covered only by the
leanchecker --fresh pass over the whole import closure, Mathlib included, which is running as this is written (about an hour; this sentence is updated when it lands).
(iii) Statement match. The theorem is a conjunction of three things, and all three are needed. GreedyExtension Seed S 97: for n ≤ 97, S n ↔ Seed n
(Seed is the 21-element disjunction, literally), and for n > 97, S n ↔ ¬PairSum S n, where PairSum X n is ∃ a b, X a ∧ X b
∧ a + b = n — equal summands allowed, nothing about order. enumeration is Nat.nth S (0-indexed), so enumeration i = 97 fixes
i = 20, the 21st term, and LeastGreedyStep enumeration j for every j ≥ 20 is a faithful encoding of Erdős–Graham's sentence: aj < aj+1,
aj+1 is not au + av for any u, v ≤ j (PrefixPairSum, equal indices allowed), and every
t strictly between is. EventuallyPeriodic f is ∃ N p, 0 < p ∧ ∀ n ≥ N, f(n+p) = f(n), applied to
gap n = Nat.nth S (n+1) − Nat.nth S n. That is the negation of the problem as the monograph, the page and Formal Conjectures state it — with one seam, now closed. The Formal Conjectures
theorem quantifies over a : ℕ → ℤ with IsLeast in ℤ and asks for the rule only eventually (a strictly broader class than Erdős–Graham's); the formal proof it links
(plby's lean-proofs, Boris Alexeev, 26 August; Lean v4.33.0 / Mathlib v4.33.0) is Li's development re-hosted byte for byte with a wrapper theorem not_erdos_341 over ℕ,
the FC file's own theorem still ends in sorry, and its docstring says "casting it to ℤ gives a counterexample" — a short lemma (ℤ against ℕ, IsLeast, the eventual quantifier), which the PR describes as "verified locally". Our referee seat
wrote that bridge: Bridge.lean states erdos_341 verbatim (with answer(False) read as False, which is all FC's answer macro
elaborates to) and proves it from Li's least_next_rule_after_fixed_seed and gap_not_eventuallyPeriodic; it compiles against Li's oleans on exactly
[propext, Classical.choice, Quot.sound], a sorry in place of the aperiodicity lemma prints sorryAx, and changing FC's sumset to Queneau's i < j
makes it fail to typecheck. We replayed all three (steps.tsv rows bridge_*). So Li's theorem now implies the community's formal statement by a kernel check on our
machine — not yet in Formal Conjectures' own tree.
(iv) A hostile referee seat with tools (Opus), briefed to refute and to quote what it objected to, separately from the host: HOLDS AS READ — nothing fatal, no gap,
ten objections, all exposition. Its own instruments, written without reading ours: a scan-based greedy (pair enumeration, not bitset shifts) that reproduces Li's set to 107,
1,224,508 terms, 0 mismatches; an FFT sumset check of t ∈ S ⇔ t ∉ S+S with 0 violations for every 97 < t ≤ 134,217,727 — through
every controller transition up to quotient 2.7·106, with perturbed sets producing 2 and 3 violations; the three controller identities on [0, 263,144) with the sumset
run-lists matching the paper's (12)–(14) including the small initial pieces; and for §5, the shield identities and |Ap| = 7p − 20 for every 18 ≤ p ≤ 400,
the greedy run directly from A18 and A19 (106 and 113 elements) agreeing with Sp to 3·106, and the recurrence for
p ∈ {18, 19, 23, 40} to 224. Two of its findings are worth a reader's attention. The threshold p ≥ 18 is sufficient, not sharp: p ∈ {12, 14, 15, 16, 17}
pass every check too, p = 13 fails the cover (18,719 recurrence violations), and the mod-49 seed is not a member of the family (p = 7 fails its shield). And the seed is
far from sum-free — twelve of its 21 elements are sums of two earlier ones (2 = 1+1, 3 = 1+2, 5 = 2+3, 7 = 2+5, 27 = 5+22, 28 = 1+27, 32 = 5+27, 40 = 13+27, 47 = 7+40, 48 = 1+47,
52 = 5+47, 63 = 27+36) — which #341 permits ("Given a set of integers"), but which means the Cameron/Calkin–Finch question for sum-free seeds (their Conjecture 4.2, the setting of every
"apparently aperiodic" set in their Table 3) is untouched. The rest: the controller proof's "direct interval calculation" never writes the cross-scale sums (true; the Lean's
pairSum_y*_iff do); D, B, P switch silently from residue sets to sets of integers and the six-type classification needs their pairwise disjointness, never stated;
the "Hence" before S ∩ [1, 97] = A needs Y0 ∩ {0, 1} = {0}, Y1 ∩ {0, 1} = {1}, {0, 1} ⊆ Y2; §5's "the argument
is the same" changes two facts (Dp + Dp ≤ 2M − 8 and Dp ∩ Qp = ∅, both true, both unstated); Dickson
is never cited; and the paper nowhere says that the result is specific to the equal-summand rule.

The convention, settled both ways — and what it does to this seed. Because Green lists two variants, we ran Li's seed under Queneau's rule too (distinct indices,
i < j). It is eventually periodic: gap period 693, membership period 5,733 from N = 3,364 (the 422nd term) — and not merely observed:
our July finite certificate (the lemma on /report/341), adapted to the distinct rule — the adaptation is one case: a residue whose only
tail representation is as a double needs two tail elements of that residue below x, so the finite check runs to 2N + 2q − 2 instead of 2N + q − 2
(our first version used the shorter cutoff; the blind read caught it) — proves it periodic forever: recurrence on the proposed periodic set through 18,192 with zero violations, and
E + R = ℤ/qℤ ∖ R with |E| = 706, |R| = 693, |E + R| = 5,040 residues. Why the convention matters: the distinct-index rule forbids
only an element added to itself, and the first integer where the two runs part is 126 = 63 + 63 — but the controller's gates are doubles at the level of residues, and losing the
self-sums is enough to break the loop and let a period settle. So after today Green's "For both variants, the following question appears to be open" reads: Dickson's variant is closed (NO), Queneau's
is open, and this seed is not a counterexample to it. The square seed, for the record: under Dickson's rule {1, 4, 9, 16, 25} is periodic from a87 = 440 with 224 terms per
1,176 (KentaKitamura's July comment on the page, and our July certificate, recurrence through 2,054); under Queneau's rule it is periodic from the very first greedy term, 27, with 10 terms per 41 (recurrence checked through 134). Erdős and Graham's
"thousands of terms" does not reproduce under either rule; we do not know what they computed. (Negative control for the certificate: fed the period-6 illusion from the last quarter of Li's
greedy run, it reports a violation at 12,845,035 and a failed residue identity.)

The world, 13 October. Proof-claims tab: Li's claim, no comments on it, no second claim. Comments tab: one, Kitamura's period-224 computation (8 July), which
disagrees with the page's "thousands of terms" and is right. Formal Conjectures: research solved since 18 September, citing "Li, Z., Counterexamples to Erdős Problem 341 (2026)" and the
plby formalization; Formal Conjectures' statements are sorry by design, so the status is a curator's judgement plus a link, not a kernel check of erdos_341. teorth
database: OPEN, last touched 31 August 2025; our board carries that database and today still says open for #341 — this entry is the correction. OpenAI's 6 October catalogue: #341 is not among its
Erdős problems. Nothing on arXiv or MathOverflow discusses the claim (the paper is not on arXiv; its DOI is Zenodo). The literature behind the page, read by the referee seat: Dickson's 1934 paper (Bull. AMS 40, 711–714) defines the greedy rule — his "construction I",
equal summands allowed, with 0 in the set — and never poses the periodicity question at all (Calkin–Erdős 1996, footnote 1: "the problem as stated by Guy doesn't appear in the reference cited");
Calkin–Finch 1996 list nineteen "apparently aperiodic" complete sum-free sets checked to 107 and say that no method is known to prove any of them aperiodic; Calkin–Finch–Flowers 2005,
Wen–Zhang–Wu 2015 and Finch's 2020 addenda add evidence, not proofs; and two September 2026 preprints by van Berkel and Bosma (arXiv:2609.16843, 2609.18522) conjecture eventual periodicity for the
strict (i < j) variant — consistent with what Li's seed does under that rule. So, to everything we and the seat could find, Li's is the first proved aperiodic
example of either kind. Provenance nits: the paper cites Zenodo v2.0.1 and the record's latest version is v2.1.0 (the Lean files are byte-identical across versions and with the GitHub commit we
built); a since-deleted BUILD_VERIFIED.md in the repository recorded a hash for Erdos341.lean that does not match the committed file — a self-report, and we built the
committed file. The claimant's own disclosure: "developed with assistance from OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol. All arguments were checked and verified by the author." A model and its user built the construction; the user and a kernel checked it; a second
kernel run, a machine referee and a machine reader now have. No human referee has, and this page is not one.

Our July report, and what is left. Twelve days before Li posted, our working report proved the finite all-future certificate used above,
certified every nonempty seed in [12] except one (4,094 of 4,095), certified {1, 4, 9, 16, 25}'s 1,176/224 tail exactly, and left E = {1, 4, 8, 10, 11, 12} with no period
q ≤ 20,000 starting by 479,993, through 500,000 — "not a proof of aperiodicity", and we did not call it a counterexample. It was the right caution: Li's counterexample is designed,
not found, and no finite run would have recognised it. What is open now is sharper than before. (1) The smallest counterexample: every seed inside [12] is periodic except possibly E,
Li's has 21 elements and reaches 97. One factor of 7 in the modulus is forced by the loop (r → 2r → 4r → 8r ≡ r needs M | 7r); the other
is Li's free parameter (§5 is modulus 7p with rails p, 2p, 4p, and the main example is p = 7, which the family's own shield rejects). Whether a shield
fits a modulus 7p with p < 7, with the same controller (it carries over whenever 4p + 4p = M + p) and a seed equal to S ∩ [1, 2M − 1],
is a finite search nobody has run. (2) Is E itself periodic? Still unknown. (3) Queneau's variant, for every seed. (4) The question for seeds that are themselves sum-free — Cameron's, as Calkin and Finch studied it — is
not touched: this seed has twelve elements that are sums of two earlier ones, no member of the family is sum-free either (1, 3, 4 lie in every Ap), and whether a sum-free
seed can generate an aperiodic set remains exactly as open as it was.
