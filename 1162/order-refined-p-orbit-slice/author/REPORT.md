## 2. Current literature and OEIS

Roney-Dougal and Tracey, *Subgroups of symmetric groups: enumeration and
asymptotic properties*, arXiv:2503.05416v1 (2025), prove **[b]**

\[
 |\operatorname{Sub}(S_n)|=2^{n^2/16+o(n^2)}.
\]

Their Theorem 1 gives the stronger two-sided bounds

\[
2^{n^2/16+\alpha n\log_2 n}
\le |\operatorname{Sub}(S_n)|
\le 2^{n^2/16+\beta n^{3/2}}
\]

for absolute constants \(\alpha>0\) and \(\beta\). Their Theorem 6 proves that
a uniformly random subgroup has a Sylow 2-subgroup of order at least
\(2^{\nu n}\), with probability tending to one, whenever
\(\nu<1/2-\sqrt3/4\). Their Proposition 7.3 uses subdirect products of
\(C_p^m\), generated by disjoint p-cycles, to obtain the lower bound. The
formula below exactly enumerates that natural slice by subgroup order and
extracts its second-order term. It is a sharpening of that construction, not
a new proof of the main \(n^2/16\) theorem.

Derek Holt, *Enumerating subgroups of the symmetric group*, Contemp. Math.
511 (2010), gives exact totals through degree 18. The current OEIS record
A005432, frozen from the official `oeisdata` export, likewise ends at

\[
f(18)=7{,}598{,}016{,}157{,}515{,}302{,}757.
\]

It should not be confused with A000638, which counts conjugacy classes rather
than all labelled subgroups and was extended to degrees 19 and 20 in 2026.
The result below does **not** determine the missing value \(f(19)\).

Exact-title, exact-formula, and citation searches through 2026-08-11 found no
later primary paper that states the order-refined formula below. This is a
search report, not a claim of literature novelty. The inclusion-exclusion
count of full-support vector subspaces is itself standard.

## 3. Exact theorem

Fix a prime \(p\). Let \(n=pm+q\), where \(m,q\geq0\), and let
\(T_p(n;m,r)\) be the number of p-subgroups \(H\leq S_n\) such that:

1. \(H\) has exactly \(m\) nontrivial orbits, each of size \(p\), and exactly
   \(q\) fixed points; and
2. \(|H|=p^r\).

Write \({d\brack r}_p\) for the Gaussian binomial coefficient. Then **[a]**

\[
\boxed{
T_p(n;m,r)=
\frac{n!}{q!\,m!\,[p(p-1)]^m}
\sum_{j=0}^{m}(-1)^j{m\choose j}{m-j\brack r}_p .
}
\tag{1}
\]

### Proof

On each nontrivial orbit, the image of a p-subgroup is a transitive p-subgroup
of \(S_p\), hence the unique type \(C_p\). The action on the union of the
orbits is faithful, so \(H\) is a subdirect subgroup of

\[
C_p^m\cong\mathbb F_p^m.
\]

Consequently \(H\) is an \(r\)-dimensional vector subspace whose projection
onto every coordinate is nonzero. Equivalently, it is contained in none of
the \(m\) coordinate hyperplanes. Inclusion-exclusion gives the sum in (1).

It remains to count the ambient products \(C_p^m\). Choose the \(q\) fixed
points, partition the rest into \(m\) unordered p-sets, and choose a regular
cyclic subgroup on each p-set. There are \((p-2)!\) such cyclic subgroups on a
fixed p-set, hence the number of ambient products is

\[
\frac{n!}{q!(p!)^m m!}\,(p-2)!^m
=\frac{n!}{q!m![p(p-1)]^m}.
\]

There is no overcount: from a full-support \(H\), its nontrivial orbits and
its projection to each orbit recover the ambient product uniquely. This
proves (1).

## 4. One exact order carries the leading exponent

Take \(r=\lfloor m/2\rfloor\). A union bound over coordinate hyperplanes gives

\[
\frac{\#\{\text{non-full-support r-spaces}\}}{{m\brack r}_p}
\le m\frac{{m-1\brack r}_p}{{m\brack r}_p}
=m\frac{p^{m-r}-1}{p^m-1}=o(1).
\]

Also \({m\brack r}_p=\Theta_p(p^{r(m-r)})\). For
\(r=\lfloor m/2\rfloor\), this and the preceding full-support estimate give

\[
\log_2\!\left(\sum_{j=0}^{m}(-1)^j{m\choose j}
{m-j\brack r}_p\right)
=\frac{\log_2p}{4}m^2+O_p(1).
\]

When \(p\mid n\), put \(m=n/p\) and \(q=0\). Stirling's formula gives the
ambient-product contribution separately as

\[
\begin{aligned}
\log_2\frac{n!}{m![p(p-1)]^m}
={}&\left(1-\frac1p\right)n\log_2 n\\
&-\left[\left(1-\frac1p\right)\log_2e
+\frac1p\log_2(p-1)\right]n+O_p(1).
\end{aligned}
\]

Adding the two displayed contributions proves **[a]**

\[
\begin{aligned}
\log_2 T_p\left(n;\frac np,\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2p}\right\rfloor\right)
={}&\frac{\log_2p}{4p^2}n^2
+\left(1-\frac1p\right)n\log_2 n\\
&-\left[\left(1-\frac1p\right)\log_2e
+\frac1p\log_2(p-1)\right]n+O_p(1).
\end{aligned}
\tag{2}
\]

In particular, for even \(n\), there are subgroups of the **single exact
order** \(2^{\lfloor n/4\rfloor}\) numbering **[a]**

\[
2^{\,n^2/16+(n/2)\log_2n-(n/2)\log_2e+O(1)}.
\tag{3}
\]

Thus the known leading exponent \(n^2/16\) is already attained at one order,
and this slice has the explicit \((n/2)\log_2n\) second term. Equation (3) is
a global lower bound for \(|\operatorname{Sub}(S_n)|\), but it does not give a
matching global second-order upper bound.

For example, at \(n=20\), formula (1) gives exactly

\[
52{,}502{,}131{,}339{,}708{,}050
\]

such subgroups of order \(2^5=32\). **[a], [d]**

## 5. A restricted statistical theorem on order

Let \(\mathcal F_{p,m}\) be the family in (1) with \(n=pm\), allowing all
ranks, and choose \(H_m\) uniformly from this family. Put
\(R_m=\log_p|H_m|\). The common ambient-product factor in (1) cancels, so the
rank law is exactly the dimension law of a uniformly chosen full-support
subspace of \(\mathbb F_p^m\).

If \(m=2s\), then for every fixed integer \(k\), **[a]**

\[
\Pr(R_m-s=k)\longrightarrow
\frac{p^{-k^2}}{\sum_{j\in\mathbb Z}p^{-j^2}}.
\tag{4}
\]

If \(m=2s+1\), then **[a]**

\[
\Pr(R_m-s=k)\longrightarrow
\frac{p^{-k(k-1)}}{\sum_{j\in\mathbb Z}p^{-j(j-1)}}.
\tag{5}
\]

Indeed, for fixed distance from the middle, full support has probability
\(1-o(1)\), while

\[
{m\brack r}_p\sim
\frac{p^{r(m-r)}}{\prod_{i\ge1}(1-p^{-i})}.
\]

The uniform bound \({m\brack r}_p=O_p(p^{r(m-r)})\) supplies a summable
theta-function majorant, proving normalization and passage to the limit.
In particular,

\[
R_m=\frac m2+O_{\Pr}(1).
\]

This is a genuine order limit law, but only for the explicitly defined
p-orbit slice \(\mathcal F_{p,m}\). It is **not** asserted for a uniformly
random subgroup of \(S_n\); the second question in problem 1162 remains open
beyond results such as Roney-Dougal--Tracey Theorem 6.
