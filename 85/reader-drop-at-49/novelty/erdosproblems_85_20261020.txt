 85 | Erdős Problems Forum Inbox Favourites Tags More FAQ Prizes Problem Lists Definitions Links Forum Menu Inbox Favourites Tags FAQ Prizes Problem Lists Definitions Links Go Go Random Let $n\geq 4$ and $f(n)$ be minimal such that every graph on $n$ vertices with minimal degree $\geq f(n)$ contains a $C_4$. Is it true that, for all large $n$, $f(n+1)\geq f(n)$? #85 : [Er93,p.345] [Er94b] [Er95] [Er96] graph theory | ramsey theory The function $f(n)$ is a reformulation of the Ramsey number $R(C_4,K_{1,n})$, in that\[R(C_4,K_{1,n})=\min\{ m : f(m)\leq m-n\}\]and\[f(n)=\min\{ m : m\geq R(C_4, K_{1,n-m})\}.\]The behaviour of this Ramsey number more generally is [552] . A weaker version of the conjecture asks for some constant $c$ such that $f(m)>f(n)-c$ for all $m>n$. This question can be asked for other graphs than $C_4$. The bounds in [552] imply in particular that $f(n)<\sqrt{n}+1$ and\[f(n)=(1+o(1))\sqrt{n}.\]It is easy to check that $f(4)=2$. (The most recent comments are highlighted in a red border.) I would expect some counterexamples appear. It turns out that $f(48)=8$. Note that $f(50)=8$ e.g. follows from the Hoffman-Singleton graph and for $\delta \ge 8$ to avoid a $C_4$ one needs $n \ge 57.$ Now the determination of $f(49)$ is tough. (colleague Tibo Van den Eede tried to verify, but it takes too long) Notice for that $49$-vertex graph with $\delta =7$ needs to have at least one vertex $v$ of degree $8$ and then locally the structure is known, except from $G[N_2(v)]$. In general, there can be expected a difference between odd and even $n$ when the minimum degree is odd in the second case. (but it does not happen always, as no small counterexample was derived) StijnC — 15:06 on 17 Sep 2026 👍 0 📝 0 🤖 0 To see why $f(n) < \sqrt{n} + 1$ holds, let $\delta$ be the minimum degree of a graph with $n$ vertices that does not contain a $C_4$ and define, for every vertex $v$, the set $S_v$ of all unordered pairs of distinct neighbours of $v$. Now, if $S_v \cap S_w \neq \emptyset$ for some distinct vertices $v$ and $w$, say $(x,y) \in S_v \cap S_w$, then $v, x, w, y$ would be a $C_4$. In order words, to prevent the graph from containing a $C_4$, we need to have that all sets $S_v$ are disjoint. Now we note that, in total, there are $\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)$ unordered pairs of vertices, while $S_v$ contains at least $\frac{1}{2} \delta (\delta-1)$ pairs for every vertex $v$. We therefore get, assuming the sets $S_v$ are pairwise disjoint, \begin{align*} \frac{1}{2}n(n-1) &\ge \sum_v |S_v| \\ &\ge \sum_v \frac{1}{2}\delta(\delta-1) \\ &= \frac{1}{2}n\delta(\delta-1). \end{align*}In other words, $n-1 \ge \delta (\delta - 1)$, so that in particular $\delta < \sqrt{n} + \frac{1}{2}$. Note that this proof would have also worked for the average instead of minimum degree. As for the lower bound $f(n) > \big(1 - o(1)\big)\sqrt{n}$, the polarity graph induced by the projective plane (see Theorem $1$ in this $1966$ paper by Erdős, Rényi and Sós for a construction) has $n = q^2 + q + 1$ vertices and minimum degree $q = \big(1 - o(1)\big)\sqrt{n}$, while it does not contain a $C_4$.
